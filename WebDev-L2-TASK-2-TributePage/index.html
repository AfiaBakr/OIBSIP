Her story

A life measured in discovery

Maria Skłodowska was born in Warsaw in 1867, in a Poland then ruled by the Russian Empire, where universities were closed to women. The youngest of five children in a family of teachers, she refused to let that barrier decide her future. She studied in secret at the underground “Flying University” and spent years working as a governess, saving money so that she and her sister could each, in turn, afford an education abroad.

In 1891 she finally arrived in Paris and enrolled at the Sorbonne, living on very little in a cold attic room while she completed degrees in both physics and mathematics. It was there that she met Pierre Curie, a quiet and brilliant physicist who shared her devotion to science. They married in 1895 and began one of the most remarkable scientific partnerships in history, working side by side in a leaky, poorly equipped shed.

Inspired by Henri Becquerel’s finding that uranium gave off mysterious rays, Marie chose those rays as the subject of her doctoral research. She showed that the radiation came from the atom itself — a radical idea at the time — and coined the term “radioactivity” to describe it. By processing tonnes of pitchblende ore by hand, the Curies discovered two new elements in 1898: polonium, named after her homeland, and radium. Her work earned a share of the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics, making her the first woman ever to receive the award.

Tragedy struck in 1906 when Pierre was killed in a street accident. Marie carried on alone, taking over his teaching post to become the Sorbonne’s first female professor, and in 1911 she won a second Nobel Prize, this time in Chemistry, for isolating radium and studying its properties. When the First World War broke out, she turned her knowledge into direct service: she designed mobile X-ray vehicles, learned to drive them, and trained women as radiology operators, helping doctors examine wounded soldiers close to the front lines.

Years of handling radioactive material without protection took a heavy toll. Marie Curie died in 1934 of aplastic anaemia, a condition almost certainly caused by her long exposure to radiation. Yet she never sought wealth from her discoveries — she deliberately chose not to patent the radium-isolation process, so that scientists everywhere could build on it freely. Her curiosity, persistence and generosity continue to inspire everyone who has ever been told a door was closed to them.

Milestones

Key moments & achievements

  1. 1867

    Born in Warsaw

    Maria Skłodowska is born on 7 November, the youngest child of two educators.

  2. 1891

    Moves to Paris

    Enrols at the Sorbonne, later earning degrees in physics (1893) and mathematics (1894).

  3. 1895

    Marries Pierre Curie

    The beginning of a lifelong scientific partnership in the study of radiation.

  4. 1898

    Two new elements

    Announces the discovery of polonium and radium, and introduces the term “radioactivity”.

  5. 1903

    Nobel Prize in Physics

    Shared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel — the first Nobel Prize awarded to a woman.

  6. 1906

    First woman professor at the Sorbonne

    After Pierre’s sudden death, she takes over his chair and continues their research.

  7. 1911

    Nobel Prize in Chemistry

    Becomes the first person to win two Nobel Prizes — and still the only one in two different sciences.

  8. 1914–18

    X-rays on the front line

    Equips mobile radiography cars, nicknamed “petites Curies”, to treat wounded soldiers in WWI.

  9. 1934

    Passes away

    Dies on 4 July in Passy, France, of aplastic anaemia linked to radiation exposure.

  10. 1995

    Honoured in the Panthéon

    Becomes the first woman laid to rest in the Paris Panthéon on her own merits.

Legacy

Why she still matters

Medicine

Her research laid the foundations for radiotherapy, now one of the main treatments for cancer.

Science for all

By refusing to patent her methods, she made her discoveries freely available to researchers worldwide.

Breaking barriers

She opened doors for generations of women in science — including her daughter Irène, a Nobel laureate herself.

Written in the table

Element 96, curium, is named in honour of Marie and Pierre Curie.
